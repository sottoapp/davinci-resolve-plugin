### 1. Why Cuts Work (02:08 – 07:42)

* **The Blink Theory (Thought Punctuation)**
  * **Principle:** Although vision feels continuous, humans blink to separate and punctuate thoughts when an idea is completed (**03:20–03:50**). In film, a shot presents an idea, and the cut functions as a blink separating those ideas (**03:51–04:00**).
  * **Cutting on Actor Blinks:** Murch noticed while cutting *The Conversation* that cuts placed at moments where Gene Hackman naturally blinked felt subconscious and complete (**04:08–04:35**).
  * **Audience Synchronization:** Finding the correct rhythm guides the audience to blink in unison with the film (**04:39–05:03**).
  * **Contrast with the "Dragnet System":** The 1950s detective show *Dragnet* cut immediately after every line of dialogue (**05:05–05:22**), which creates a punchy rhythm but lacks conversational nuance (**05:23–05:42**).
  * *Concrete timeline action:* Slicing the clip at the exact frame an actor initiates a blink or finishes a thought unit rather than mechanically cutting at dialogue endpoints.

* **Dream Logic & Juxtaposition**
  * **Principle:** Cuts resemble the abrupt, non-linear juxtaposition of images experienced in dreams (**05:43–06:18**). In a dark cinema, viewers accept visual discontinuity because it mirrors dream logic (**06:19–06:33**).
  * **Kuleshov Effect:** Combining two separate images to generate a third distinct meaning (**06:34–06:48**).
  * *Concrete timeline action:* Juxtaposing two disparate shots on the timeline sequentially to produce symbolic meaning.

* **The Beehive Analogy & The 30-Degree Rule**
  * **Principle:** Bees tolerate their hive being moved miles away, but moving it just two yards leaves them fatally disoriented because the environment appears almost unchanged (**06:50–07:11**). Similarly, cutting to a shot too similar in angle or focal length feels jarring (a jump cut) (**07:12–07:20**).
  * **Rule:** When cutting between consecutive shots of the same subject, change camera angle by at least 30 degrees (**07:21–07:35**).
  * *Concrete timeline action:* Selecting and placing coverage shots where the camera axis shifts $\ge 30^\circ$ relative to the preceding shot.

### 2. Murch's Rule of Six (07:44 – 11:10)

Shown directly on a page from *In the Blink of an Eye* (**07:52**, **10:50**) and in animated pie charts (**07:56**, **09:12**, **10:30**), Murch ranks the six criteria of an edit by order of priority and assigned weights:

1. **Emotion (51%)** (**08:00 – 08:32**)
   * *Principle:* The primary goal of an editor; must be preserved at all costs. The cut must reflect what the audience should feel at that exact narrative juncture.
   * *Conceptual action:* Retaining a take or cut point that captures the performance's emotional truth, even if it creates technical continuity errors.
2. **Story (23%)** (**08:33 – 08:50**)
   * *Principle:* The cut must actively push the narrative forward and clarify character motivations and plot developments.
   * *Conceptual action:* Trimming footage that does not progress character intention or plot momentum.
3. **Rhythm (10%)** (**08:51 – 09:12**)
   * *Principle:* The cut must hit on the right musical beat (neither "rushing nor dragging").
   * *Conceptual action:* Adjusting head/tail trim durations frame-by-frame to align with the scene's internal cadence or audio beat.
   * *Tightly Bound Core:* Murch notes the top three (Emotion, Story, Rhythm = 84%) are bound together like protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus (**09:13–09:33**).
4. **Eye-Trace (7%)** (**09:34 – 10:02**)
   * *Principle:* Managing where the viewer’s gaze lands on screen so attention seamlessly transitions into the focal area of the incoming shot.
   * *Conceptual action:* Framing and timing cuts so the center of interest matches the spatial quadrant where the audience was looking in the prior shot (demonstrated visually with crosshairs at **09:38–10:00**).
5. **Two-Dimensional Plane of Screen / Planarity (5%)** (**10:03 – 10:18**)
   * *Principle:* Respecting screen direction and the 180-degree line of action established by the setup.
   * *Conceptual action:* Ensuring incoming shots do not cross the stage line unless an intentional axis jump is required.
6. **Three-Dimensional Space of Action (4%)** (**10:19 – 10:30**)
   * *Principle:* Maintaining physical continuity and relational positioning of characters and objects in the room.
   * *Conceptual action:* Matching spatial blocking across different camera setups.

* **Hierarchy Rule:** Satisfying higher-order criteria (Emotion, Story, Rhythm) will make viewers overlook flaws in lower-order criteria (Eye-trace, Planarity, 3D Continuity), but satisfying lower-order criteria will never salvage an emotionally or narratively dead edit (**10:30–11:00**).

### 3. Practical Editing Tenets (Chapter 3: An Editor's Job, 11:10 – 14:15)

* **Tenet 1: Have a Plan & Pick Representative Stills (11:30 – 11:50)**
  * *Seen/Heard:* When screening dailies, Murch pulls a representative still frame from every setup/take and displays them across large boards (**11:36–11:43**, **14:00**).

* **Tenet 2: Stand While Editing (11:51 – 12:15)**
  * *Seen/Heard:* Footage shows Murch working at an elevated, standing desk (**11:48–12:08**). He states editing is a "crystallized dance" and standing allows him to internalize visual rhythms (**11:58–12:14**).

* **Tenet 3: Active Creative Collaboration with the Director (12:15 – 12:39)**
  * *Seen/Heard:* Archival footage shows Murch collaborating with Francis Ford Coppola (**12:18–12:30**). While editors must serve the director's overarching vision, directors often have blind spots regarding smaller details, requiring editors to proactively propose creative solutions.

* **Tenet 4: Protect Audience Objectivity by Staying Off Set (12:40 – 13:09)**
  * *Heard (Murch quote):* Murch avoids visiting the shooting set or seeing actors out of costume (**12:50–13:08**).
  * *Purpose:* Prevents on-set difficulties, weather compromises, or personal biases from clouding the editor's judgment, ensuring they experience the footage purely as an audience member will.
  * *Concrete action:* Evaluating clips strictly from the imported media files without consulting on-set production anecdotes.

* **Tenet 5: Diagnose Test Audience Feedback Correctly (13:10 – 13:55)**
  * *Principle:* Audiences are reliable at detecting *that* something is not working, but unreliable at identifying *why* (**13:20–13:30**).
  * *Application:* If 80% of test audiences dislike a specific scene, the impulse is to trim or remove it (**13:30–13:40**). However, the scene itself is usually fine; the true issue is typically that the audience was missing a crucial narrative setup earlier in the film (**13:40–13:53**).
  * *Concrete timeline/software action:* Addressing scene complaints by adjusting or restoring narrative context/exposition in earlier scenes on the timeline rather than deleting the flagged scene.
